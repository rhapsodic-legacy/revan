## Chapter 1: ASHE. "Recruit"

> *Told in Ashe's own voice, first person, following Jesse's passage. Jesse's paragraphs are verbatim (three typos fixed: "more an" → "more than", "upon" → "up on", "In idealist" → "An idealist").*

Quiet morning. I like a quiet morning. Quiet means nobody's bleeding yet.

I had my arm shoulder-deep in a wall panel in the workshop, chasing a coupling that's been rattling loose since the last port. Deena'd skin me for touching her ship. She can try. A man who can't fix his own transport don't deserve to ride in it.

My sword was on the bench behind me. Plain claymore. Straight steel, no enamel, no gold, none of the jewellery the Srov hang off their blades so everybody knows how special they are. Oiled it this morning. Dressed the edge last night. Looks like it came off the anvil yesterday, and that's the point. You want to know what kind of soldier a man is, look at his kit. A man who lets his weapon go to rust is going to let his people go to rust right behind it.

---

I was a soldier before any of this. Galactic Unity. When the Rebels rose up, I went where they pointed me and I put the insurrection down, one dirty world at a time. I got good at it. I got hit, too, and hit bad. Last thing I remember of that war is the ground coming up to meet me. Next thing, I'm waking up in a Srov healing ward with a robe at the foot of the bed and some old Seer telling me I've got Sight.

Twelve years, near enough.

I'll tell you what I know now that I didn't know then. The Unity's rotten. Top to bottom. I've been a merc long enough to see it from the cheap seats: the bribes, the rent-a-goons in nice armour, the worlds they bleed dry and call it order. Maybe the Rebels had a point. Maybe they didn't. Either way, I ain't ever putting that uniform on again. I'll take pay from just about anybody. Not them.

---

The Srov taught me what they could. Taught me Sight. Taught me how to light a blade and how to sit still for six hours chanting about the light while the galaxy burned outside the window.

What they couldn't teach me was how to stop being a soldier.

There was a boy in that temple. Scrawny. Big dark grey eyes, always watching. Dust of the Sun, they called him. Fancy name for a kid who couldn't keep his robes clean. He'd show up at the edge of the training yard every time I was out there, and after a while I let him spar. He was quick. He was smart, smarter than me, honest to God, and he didn't know it yet. He looked at me like I'd hung the stars.

You can't let a kid look at you like that. It'll get him killed. So I knocked him down every time he got up, and every time he got up again.

Five years ago the Srov decided my methods weren't the methods of the order. Master Ondren stood in the hangar with his hands tucked in his sleeves, face all arranged into regret.

"Your methods, Brother Ashe, are not the methods of the order."

"My methods brought eleven of your people home breathing from a mess your methods made. You're welcome."

"You executed the prisoners."

"I executed the men who'd have cut those eleven throats the second we turned our backs. And I'll do it again. Cheaper, now I'm off your payroll."

They called it an indefinite sabbatical. The Srov got a gift for saying *get out* in a voice like *bless you*.

I walked up my ramp and didn't look back.

---

Six hours out, I heard something in the cargo hold that wasn't cargo.

I saw the scrawny kid, sitting behind the crates lookin' all scared with those large dark grey eyes.

"Get up recruit. Now."

I knew there was no telling him to go back.

He stood, nodded and didn't say anything.

"You still got your sword?" I asked the kid.

He nodded and actually smiled, pulling his curved two hander out of a black bag behind the crates.

"You snuck that off base? Impressive." I said.

Damned Srov don't let you go anywhere with a sword without their saying so.

He told me about some impressive ploy, crawling through a duct so detectors didn't pick the weapon up on. It explained why he was covered in dust and grease.

"I happen to need a number two right now, someone who can watch my back. You got Sight. So the spot's open for you, until I find someone better." I said.

I wanted to clap the kid on the shoulder and congratulate him for having the balls to get away from the meddlesome Srov. But he needed to toughen up fast. This little stripling with those wide innocent eyes won't survive Merc' life more than a couple weeks if he doesn't realise how unfriendly most of space is.

He was yammering about how he'd be the best number two ever.

I cut him off. "Great. Show me, don't tell me. You can have the small room by the cockpit. It's the only other 1-bunk'er besides mine. Besides us there are exactly zero other crew for now. I'll hire some once we get some cred's....

That was what, five years ago? Hard to believe this punk brat is turning into a man. An idealist hero with a death wish for standing up for the weak, but a damned fine warrior with the blade.

---

The Srov sent messages for a year. *Return the novice.* Then they gave up and sent one naming me his Master, like a title fixes anything.

"One must be a master," I told the screen, "to be someone's Master." Then I switched it off.

I never called myself that. I call him recruit, because that's what he is. Putting on the uniform doesn't make you a soldier. Getting tested does, and the testing doesn't stop.

---

And that's what's chewing on me this morning, worse than the coupling.

We're making a name. Fine. A name brings work, and work pays. But a name brings eyes too, and some of those eyes belong to folks who'd gut a nineteen-year-old idealist for the change in his pockets and sleep fine after.

The recruit's good. Better than good. I've watched him think through a firefight like it was a puzzle box. He reads a room, reads a deal, reads a liar before the liar's done lying. He's started giving orders without noticing, and the scary part is people are starting to follow them.

But he still hesitates. Half a second, when a man raises a weapon, where the recruit's still looking for the version where nobody dies. Space'll find that half-second. It'll find it, crawl in, and take him apart from the inside. Maybe not today. But one day I won't be standing behind him to finish what he hesitated to start.

He's got to get meaner. He's got to learn the man in front of you ain't owed a second chance just 'cause you got one to spare. And if he's going to run his own crew someday, I need to know if he wants it.

---

The contract was on the bench by the sword. Srov seal on it. **Sixty thousand credits.** A rescue. A princess.

I read it twice. Liked it less the second time.

Princess Melonsi Ananna of Seonha. Master of the order, for whatever that's worth. I've crossed her path a handful of times in those white corridors, long enough to get scolded, whipped, or walked past. She's the Srov in miniature: beautiful, rich, born certain she's right, with a thousand-year-old institution behind her telling her she is. Meddling control-freak Srov, and she's the shiniest jewel in their crown.

I'll take their money. Money don't care whose hand it was in last.

And she's useful. I'll give her that. Those curves of hers, that salty, beautiful face. Men'd walk off a cliff for one smile out of that. Men *have*. So here's the question I keep turning over, colder than any contract:

*Can the recruit see past it?*

If not her, then the next vixen. Or the one after that. Galaxy's full of beautiful things that'll kill you if you let 'em, and that boy's still young enough to let 'em.

---

I pulled my arm out of the panel. Coupling seated. Wiped the grease on a rag and looked at the door just as it hissed open.

The recruit came in with his hair wrecked and his jacket half on, carrying two mugs. Held one out.

I took it. Didn't say thank you. Didn't need to.

*Time to find out.*

"So," I said, leaning back on the bench. "Sixty thousand credits. What are you going to do with your cut, recruit? Buy your own ship?"

He took a long sip, like he'd already thought about it. Like he'd thought about it a lot.

## Chapter 2: DUST. "The Brains Behind the Brawn"

Dust had been awake for an hour before he got up. He'd been thinking.

About the job, mostly. Sixty thousand credits for a rescue was too much money. He'd turned the number over all night like a stone in his pocket. Nobody paid that much for a straightforward job, which meant it wasn't one. Either the Srov expected them to die, or there was a piece of the story they weren't being told. He'd fallen asleep somewhere around the fourth version of the answer and woken up with a fifth.

About the princess, a little. He'd looked at the photos in the brief, because you look at the target. She was... yeah. He'd felt his ears go hot, alone in his own bunk, which was ridiculous. Then he'd caught himself wondering how a Srov Master with an army of royal guards had gone missing on a Unity world in the first place, and that turned out to be a much more interesting question.

And about a racing pod he'd seen in a port window two weeks ago. Seriously beautiful lines. Totally impractical. He'd thought about it more than he'd admit.

He put the datapad face-down and got up.

---

When he was seven, he'd woken in the temple medbay with a bandage on his head and a healer telling him there'd been a cave-in. The Srov told him he'd been lucky. He believed them. Why wouldn't he?

The temple on Ithrael was beautiful the way a cage made of gold is beautiful. White stone, dawn prayers, masters who smiled while they told you no. They taught him to kindle a blade, and when his flame came up sooty gold with a black heart, the masters exchanged a look that Dust spent years trying to forget.

He spent most of those years at windows, watching ships lift off the landing fields, toward a galaxy that was out there *happening* without him. People in trouble. Worlds that needed somebody. The Srov were supposed to be the ones who helped, and they mostly seemed to be the ones who held meetings about helping.

And then there was Ashe.

Ashe didn't belong, and everybody knew it. He walked the cloisters like they were a parade ground. He carried a plain steel claymore when every other Srov carried something that looked designed by a jeweller with a grudge. He said what he thought, out loud, to masters' faces. When Dust started turning up at the edge of the training yard, Ashe let him spar. Knocked him down every single time. Never once told him to stop getting up.

Dust learned more in that yard than in seven years of prayers. He'd have followed Ashe into a sun.

So when they sent Ashe away, he did the next best thing.

He'd planned it for two days. Detectors on every exit picked up kindling blades, so he went through the ventilation instead, a long crawl through a duct with Kindling in a black bag dragging behind him, and came out in the hangar filthy with dust and grease. He hid behind the crates in Ashe's hold and tried not to breathe too loud.

Six hours out, the hold door opened.

"Get up recruit. Now."

He got up. Ashe asked if he still had his sword, and Dust couldn't help it, he smiled and pulled it out of the bag. Ashe said *impressive*. Dust replayed that word for about a week.

"I happen to need a number two right now," Ashe said. "Someone who can watch my back. You got Sight. So the spot's open for you, until I find someone better."

Dust had started telling him how he'd be the best number two in the galaxy, and Ashe had cut him off: *"Great. Show me, don't tell me."*

He'd been showing him ever since.

---

Five years of it. Rough ports, cheap gigs, and all the things the Srov never would have taught him. How to read a bad deal before you sign it. How a man's hands move before he lies. How to fight with a blade that isn't for ceremony. He'd chipped Kindling's guard in a bar fight on some backwater and never fixed it, because it reminded him he'd won.

He'd been wrong, too. The race, mostly. He'd seen the fix from three moves away and played the gangers who owned that chrome transport like a cheap synth. He'd won the ship. Then it turned out they'd *let* him win, so they could pin their own murders on him. Thirteen counts. Ashe got them out, and there was a planet in the galaxy neither of them could ever set foot on again.

They kept the ship. Somebody scratched out half the name and painted new letters, and now she was the *Galactic C*. Dust loved her. He also never walked her corridors without remembering he still had a lot to learn.

---

Here was the thing nobody understood, not even Ashe.

Everyone thought Ashe was looking after Dust. Sure. Ashe was the best fighter Dust had ever seen. But Ashe solved every problem by going straight through it. He made a decision and then bent the universe around it, and most of the time the universe bent.

*Most* of the time.

Somebody had to be thinking about the other times. Somebody had to see the trap before Ashe walked into it with his sword out, and talk them out of the corners Ashe was perfectly happy to fight his way out of.

The brains behind the brawn. Dust would never say it out loud. Ashe would give him that long flat look that meant *show me, don't tell me.*

So he didn't want his own ship. Every merc his age dreamed of a captain's chair. But his own ship meant Ashe on one deck and Dust on another, and nobody watching Ashe's back. *No way.*

What he wanted was a better ship. One they could both stand on, fast enough to outrun trouble and strong enough to take the jobs that actually helped people.

---

He passed Deena in the corridor on the way to the galley. She was elbow-deep in a junction box, swearing at it tenderly.

"Morning, Deena."

"Don't talk to me, I'm in a relationship with this capacitor and it's going badly." She glanced up and smiled at him, slow and warm, the way she sometimes did. "You look cute when you haven't slept, you know that?"

Dust froze. His face went hot. His mouth opened, and absolutely nothing came out of it.

Deena laughed and went back to her capacitor.

He walked the rest of the way to the galley wondering what he was supposed to have said.

---

He poured two mugs of the black sludge Ashe called coffee and carried them to the workshop. Ashe was leaning on the bench with grease up to the shoulder, the plain claymore lying behind him like it had been forged that morning.

Dust held out one mug. Ashe took it without a word. From Ashe, that was as good as a thank-you.

"So," Ashe said. "Sixty thousand credits. What are you going to do with your cut, recruit? Buy your own ship?"

Dust took a long sip. He already knew the answer. He'd known it for five years.

## Chapter 3: DEENA. "Black Hole, Warm Bathwater"

The capacitor in junction box six was dying, and Deena Ateray was the only one on board who cared.

"Shh, shh, I know," she told it, coaxing a scorched lead out with the tips of her pliers. "I know. You're tired. You've been holding up the whole aft lighting array since before Dust learned to shave, and nobody thanks you. *I* thank you. You're a good capacitor. You're the *best* capacitor."

It sparked at her. She sighed.

"Fine. Be that way. Everyone on this ship is emotionally unavailable."

---

She'd been someone's property, once.

She didn't like to think about it, so she thought about it in pieces. The lounge, all gold and velvet and smoke. The man who owned it, and who owned her. The way she'd learned to laugh at exactly the right time, and to fade out of sight at exactly the right time, and to fix the lounge's ancient, beautiful jukebox when it broke. That last part was the only part that was hers. She'd taken that jukebox apart and put it back together a hundred times, because machines did what you asked if you understood them, and nothing else in her life did.

Then two men walked in one night. A tall one with a plain sword and eyes like the edge of it, and a skinny teenager trying very hard to look like the tall one.

She'd poured their drinks. The tall one had looked at her for a long time, not the way men usually looked at her. Like he was doing math.

"How much?" he'd asked her owner.

She'd thought he meant for the night. He didn't.

She never found out exactly how much Ashe paid. She did find out that the next morning her former owner had a very long conversation with Ashe and Dust in a back room, and when he came out, he'd decided he'd never heard of her and never would again. He was quite pale. Dust had been grinning.

Ashe had put her on his ship, handed her a wrench, and said, "Engine's making a noise. Make it stop."

She'd made it stop. She'd been making things stop, and start, and sing, ever since.

---

She loved them. That was the problem.

She loved this ugly, glorious, rust-bitten ship with its chrome trim and its flip-up headlights and Lady Dusk crooning *"Good morning, darlings"* over the PA. She loved K'saabu, who'd turned up in the cargo hold one day like weather and simply *stayed*, and who followed her around like a seven-foot guard dog with a cannon. She loved Statda, who'd made one smooth, gorgeous, perfectly polite pass at her two years ago, taken "no" with a grin and a bow, and never once made it weird. And she loved Ashe and Dust.

But loving everybody was a lonely thing, out here in the black, when nobody was loving you back the way you wanted.

She wasn't stupid about it. She knew what she wanted: somebody to come home to. Somebody to argue with about ship parts at two in the morning, and then not argue with. Someone warm. The galaxy was enormous, and she was stuck on a passenger liner with five people, two of whom were hot-blooded Srov types with swords and secrets.

*And neither of them is an option. Not really.*

Ashe.

She'd thought about it. Of course she had. Everyone who'd ever met Ashe had thought about it. He was handsome the way cliffs are handsome. He'd bought her freedom without asking for a thing in return, which was more than any man had ever done. He made filthy remarks about her backside, and she gave as good as she got, and there was something in the way he *meant* none of it that she'd come to rely on.

But dating Ashe would be like diving into a black hole. There was so much more to him than he let anyone see. She'd catch it in the quiet moments: the way he went still when nobody was talking, the way his eyes went somewhere else, somewhere cold and far away. He never talked about the army. Never talked about whatever he'd done in it, or whatever had been done to him. Old wounds, she figured. Scar tissue. Whatever happened to him back then had carved out a place inside him, and she'd never be able to fill it, and she'd fall in and never come out.

And Dust.

Dust was sweet. Dust was warm bathwater. Not bad. Never bad. Comfortable. But he was a *kid*, she thought: an eager, earnest kid who blushed when she teased him, and she'd known him since he was barely tall enough to reach the top bunk. She knew him too well to be curious about him.

*Do I, though?* She didn't let herself chase that thought. It was easier to think she had Dust figured out.

She sighed, and the capacitor sparked at her again.

"Don't you start."

---

Today's plan: today was supposed to be simple. Today she was going to finish the overhaul of the forward shield emitter before they made orbit at Truevivor III, swap out junction box six, triple-check the landing struts, because rescue jobs always ended in fast exits, and maybe, *maybe*, have ten minutes to herself in the observation lounge with a cup of tea and the planet turning below.

She'd even planned what she'd say to the princess when they found her. Something cool. Something professional. Not *Wow, are those real?*, which was what every other person on the ship was apparently thinking.

She heard Statda's boots pounding down the corridor overhead, then voices: Statda's, sharp, and Ashe's, a low unbothered rumble.

The deck plating under her knees trembled. Just slightly. The kind of tremble you feel through a hull when something big goes off a long way away.

Deena put her pliers down very slowly.

"Oh no," she said to the capacitor. "Oh, no, no, no. I know that sound. That's a shield sound. That's my *shield* sound."

The ship trembled again, harder.

So much for tea.

## Chapter 4: K'SAABU. "The Straight Line"

The cannon had been cleaned twice. K'saabu cleaned it a third time.

Not because it needed it. Because today was a good day, and on a good day a warrior honours his weapon.

*Today we go to rescue someone. Where there is someone to rescue, there is someone who took them. Where there is someone who took them, there is someone who needs to be shot.*

He rumbled low in his throat. It was, he would admit to no one, excitement.

---

The galaxy had always mistaken K'saabu for a simple creature. Large, so, slow. Green, so, a beast. Loud, so, stupid.

He did not mind. To be underestimated is to be handed the first strike.

Long ago, men had come to his people's waters with chains. They thought his people were animals too: big, slow, loud. They were wrong. K'saabu had learned in those years what every great general learns too late or not at all. *Know your enemy. Know yourself. Then do not hesitate.* He had known them. He had known himself. And he had not hesitated. Not once.

There had been a great deal of blood. Enough that, even now, in quiet moments, he could still smell it.

They captured him in the end. Even a storm is caught by a mountain eventually. They put him in the arena, because a beast that kills is worth money if you sell tickets. He fought there for years. He won every fight. He learned from every one.

And he learned something else in the arena that he did not like. He liked it. The killing. Not the necessity of it, the *doing* of it. There was a red place inside him that hummed when he fought, and it was very, very hungry, and some days it was hard to tell where K'saabu ended and the hunger began.

That was the part he never spoke of. Not even to Deena.

---

How he came to the *Galactic C* is his own business. He arrived. He stayed. No one has asked him to leave, and anyone who tries will need a bigger ship.

He had nowhere else to go. He did not mind that either. He lived one day at a time. Yesterday was dead and tomorrow was not yet born. There was only today, the cannon, and the people he had chosen.

Deena, first. Always Deena. She had looked at him the first day and seen not a beast but someone in need of feeding, and a wrap for the old chain scar on his wrist. She'd torn a strip off her own scarf to do it. He still wore it. He would wear it until he died, and if he died in battle, someone had better bury him in it.

Ashe, whom he worshipped. Ashe was the only being K'saabu had ever fought and lost to. Three times. The third time, Ashe had sat down on K'saabu's chest, breathing hard, and said: "You're good. You're wasting it." K'saabu had thought about that for a month.

Dust, the cub. Dust needed to grow claws, and nobody would teach him except K'saabu, so K'saabu shoved him, mocked him, and left him to fight his own way out of corners. Dust thought it was cruelty. It was the opposite.

Statda. She was strange. She was the only one on the ship who *listened* when he spoke. The others heard the croak and the grunt and smiled and patted his arm and said, *too violent, big guy.* Statda would tilt her head behind those ridiculous glasses and say, "Okay. Walk me through it." And then she'd nod, slowly, like she was writing it down somewhere.

He respected her for that more than she knew.

---

The problem with his friends was simple. Every problem has a straight line through it. They insisted on walking around.

The straight line was usually the shortest, the bloodiest, and the most certain. *If your enemy hides in a fortress, take the fortress. If your enemy shoots at you from a station, take the station.* The crew called this "violent." K'saabu called it "finished."

He had already, in his head, planned this rescue.

The princess was on a planet. The planet would have guards. The guards would have a commander, and the commander would have a station, a base, a fortress, a house. K'saabu would take the house. Then the guards would have no commander, and the princess would have no captors. Simple.

He had explained this to Deena over breakfast. Deena had said, "Sweetie, no," patted his hand, and gone back to her tea.

He had explained it to Ashe. Ashe had said, "No." Ashe did not pat his hand. That was respect.

He had explained it to Statda. Statda had said, "Huh. You know, that's not insane." That was why he liked her.

---

Today's plan: today, he would stand at the cargo door when they landed. He would carry the cannon. He would look very large. Most of the time, looking very large was enough, and nobody needed to be shot.

Most of the time.

He hoped this would not be one of those times.

The deck trembled under his feet, the long low shudder of something big going off a long way away. Then another. Closer.

K'saabu's throat sac swelled, slowly.

"Grrrrrrohk," he said, to no one in particular, and it sounded a great deal like a man saying *finally*.

He picked up the cannon.

## Chapter 5: STATDA. "Terrible Ideas, Excellently Executed"

Statda would like the record to show that the plan had been *brilliant*.

The plan had been: land on Truevivor III in the guise of a mild-mannered independent apothecary, which she was, set up a little stall for an hour in the market district while the boys did their brooding-swordsman thing, and sell off the crate of Altenian mood-tonic she'd quietly picked up at their last port for a song. On Truevivor, that tonic went for six times the price. Two hours of honest trade, a fat profit, and back to the ship before anyone noticed she'd gone.

The record should also show that she'd said that about the last four plans, and Ashe had had to come and get her out of three of them.

*Four. Fine. Four.*

---

She'd met Ashe on a job that paid badly and ended worse. He needed a medic who could also shoot, which was a small pool, and she needed money, which was a large one. She'd come in wearing the coveralls and the glasses, because she always did, and he'd looked her over the way most men did, then a little longer than most men did, and said:

"You're hired. Glasses are a nice touch. You need 'em?"

"Not even slightly."

"Good. Then you'll see the guy who's about to shoot me."

He'd been the first man in years to see through the glasses on the first look. It annoyed her for a week.

The glasses were a tool. People saw a woman in thick lenses and baggy coveralls and decided she was harmless, a bit fuzzy, easily rounded down on a trade. Then she'd take them off. Then she'd take them for everything they had. It was the closest thing she had to a magic trick, and she wasn't a Seer, so she had to make do.

---

The job went sideways. Of course it did. Her side-scheme was to pocket a little extra cargo from the client, a fair cut by her reckoning. It turned into her backed into a corner of a warehouse with six angry men between her and the door.

Then the wall came in.

Not the door. The *wall*. Ashe walked through the hole, sword out, not in a hurry, like a man walking into a bar he didn't think much of. Dust came in behind him, eyes everywhere. The angry men stopped being angry rather quickly after that.

When it was done, Ashe looked at her, looked at the crate of goods she'd been trying to steal, and looked back at her.

"That was the dumbest damn thing I've ever seen a smart woman do."

"I had it handled."

"You had it handled like a fish has a hook handled." He wiped his blade. "You're good with the rifle. Better with the medkit. Terrible with plans. Stay on, and maybe I'll keep you from getting killed."

"Is that a job offer, Cap'?"

"It's a pity offer. It just happens to pay."

She stayed.

---

A while later, the pay stopped between gigs. The *Galactic C* didn't always have the money. Ashe told her flatly she was free to walk off at any port she liked.

She didn't walk.

When anyone asked, she told them the truth, or a version of it. She stayed for the Srov. She'd never met anything like them. Ashe and Dust, with their swords that lit up on command, their talk of Eternity and Sight, the way Ashe sometimes knew a shot was coming before the shooter did. The rules, the order, the whole mad, beautiful, rigid system of it. A girl could study that for a lifetime and not run out of questions.

That was the version she told. The truth was, she didn't know why she stayed. She'd sat up some nights trying to work it out, and the best she'd ever come up with was: *because I haven't left yet.*

---

She liked the crew, though she'd never be caught saying so with a straight face.

Deena: gorgeous, brilliant, totally oblivious to how gorgeous and brilliant she was. Statda had made her move once, early, with all the grace and charm she could muster. Deena had blushed to her coral roots and said, "Oh, Stat, I'm so flattered, but I'm, I just don't, *oh*, you're so sweet." And that was that. She wasn't an idiot. She knew a no, even a lovely one. They'd been friends ever since, which was arguably better.

Ashe: impossible, infuriating, remorseless. He knew perfectly well she had no interest in him, which apparently made it open season. He made at least one filthy remark about her every day, purely for sport. She had started rating them out of ten.

Dust: a sweetheart with a laser sword. Also a puzzle. He blushed every time she so much as smiled at him, so he was clearly into women. But he never looked. Not at her backside, not at her chest, nothing. One day, purely as an experiment, she'd asked him to rate her out of ten.

"A stunning twelve," he'd said, perfectly calm, and gone back to cleaning his sword.

Then he'd turned red to the ears. But he still didn't look. She'd been trying to work it out ever since. *(Jesse: he's "playing it cool", since hitting on someone never works with Deena, so he's trying the opposite approach with Statda. Statda doesn't know that.)*

K'saabu. Everyone else heard a big, scary, violent frog. Statda heard a strategist. Underneath the croaks and the cannon was a mind that saw the whole board at once: who wanted what, and where the soft points were. His conclusions were insane, of course. *Commandeer the space station* was not a plan for a five-person crew. But his reasoning was sound, and sound reasoning was rare on this ship. She told him so. He'd looked at her for a long time afterwards, and since then he'd stood a little closer to her in fights. She didn't know what that meant in frog. She liked it anyway.

---

She was in the med bay that morning, restocking dressings and pricing her mood-tonic in her head, when she glanced out the porthole and saw it.

A craft. Small, fast, burning hard on an intercept vector. And beyond it, from the orbital stations around Truevivor III, bright stuttering lines of defence fire, lancing up into the black.

*Pirates,* she thought. *Pirates chasing us, and the planet's shooting at them.*

The hull hummed with a distant concussion.

She ran.

Down the corridor, past the galley, and there were the boys in the workshop, having their little captain-and-protégé heart-to-heart over mugs of tar, like the universe wasn't currently on fire outside the window.

"Hey Cap', some craft is closing with us and planetary ass hats are firing at them... don't you guys hear the explosions?"

Ashe didn't even straighten up.

"The orbital defence guns aren't aimed at us, and dumb ass pirates trying to board us this close to a planet aren't something I'm worried about. They'll bounce off our shields."

*Of course.* "I'm always grateful you're the captain, cap'," she said, sweet as poison, and sprinted on toward the cockpit.

Behind her, she heard Dust's voice: "What does she want us to do, knock the pirates out of the sky? Let the planet defence guns do that."

"I heard that you wiener!" she yelled back, without slowing down.

It was only when she reached the cockpit door, and the next explosion bloomed close enough to rattle the viewports, that she realised two things.

The first was that the defence fire wasn't aimed at the pirates.

The second was that her rifle was still in the med bay.
